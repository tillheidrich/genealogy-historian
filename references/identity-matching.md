# Does this record belong to our person?

Most errors in genealogy are wrong matches: a record of a namesake attached to an ancestor. Decide every match explicitly.

## 1. Identifiers

Compare the record with what is already established, point by point:

| Identifier | Weight | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Full birth date | strong | Off by one day or one year happens in copies; note it |
| Birthplace | strong | Check historical name, spelling variants, parish vs. village |
| Parents or spouse named | very strong | The best single discriminator |
| Residence at the time | medium to strong | Street-level match is strong |
| Occupation | medium | Can change; compatible changes are fine |
| Age stated | weak | Often wrong by several years |
| Surname spelling | weak | Varies by clerk; misreadings common |
| Given name | medium | Watch call names vs. baptismal names, translations (Eduard, Edouard, Edward) |
| Religion, nationality | medium | Useful against namesakes |
| Context (event fits time and place of the life) | medium | A record in a place the person could not have been is a strong negative |

## 2. Procedure

1. List what the record says.
2. List what you know about the person, with levels.
3. Mark each identifier as match, compatible, conflict or unknown.
4. Ask: how many other people could this be? Estimate from population, name frequency and period. A common name in a large city needs more identifiers than a rare name in a village.
5. Look for an independent second record that links the two (for example a card that names the same camp, a register that names the same parents).
6. Decide and write down the reasoning in two or three sentences.

## 3. Decision guide

- **proven**: several strong identifiers match (for example name, full birth date and birthplace, or name and parents), no conflict.
- **very likely**: name plus one strong identifier and context match; one weak identifier conflicts (for example age off by five years in a newspaper); no candidate namesake found in a source that would show one.
- **possible**: name and context match, strong identifiers missing.
- **unclear**: conflicting strong identifiers, or a namesake exists in the same place and time.

## 4. Example (anonymised)

A 1916 newspaper reports a German internee "Edouard R., 26 years" in a French camp; a second paper calls him "20 years, baker". Known: the ancestor was born 1890, was a civilian internee in that camp system (prisoner cards with full birth date and birthplace), lived in Paris before the war (family story). The Red Cross card index has only one German civilian of that name.

Assessment: name, nationality, camp system and Paris match; one age is close (26 for 25), the other is wrong. No namesake in the index. Result: very likely, not proven, because the newspapers give no birth date or birthplace.

## 5. Spelling and transcription errors

Expect and test:

- OCR confusions in Fraktur: R/P, B/V, ü/u/ii, ck/k, f/ſ (long s), rn/m, c/e, 3/8, 1/l.
- Clerks writing names by ear: Schäfer, Schafer, Schaefer, Schefer; Krüger, Kruger, Krieger; Lehmann as Lehrnann or Lchmann in OCR; a name heard as "Pickert" for "Rickert".
- Foreign clerks: German umlauts dropped, given names translated or replaced by the nearest local form.

Search the variants, then read the scan.

## 6. Recording the match

For each attached record keep: what it says, which identifiers matched, which conflicted, confidence, the reasoning sentence, date of the decision. A later researcher must be able to undo the match.

Template (one block per match):

```
Match: <record> → <person>
Identifiers that match: name, birth date 15.07.1890, birthplace, address …
Identifiers that conflict: age given 20 (expected 25) …
Namesakes checked: <where, how>; result
Confidence: proven / very likely / possible / unclear
Reasoning: one or two sentences
Decided: <date>, by <who>
```

## 7. Matching by address or surname only

A shared address, a shared surname in the same village or the same application date makes a family link plausible, not proven. Record the relationship as H and name the other people of that surname in the place. Villages often had several unrelated families of the same name.

## 8. Identifying people on photos

- Who identified the person, when, and on what basis (inscription, family knowledge, resemblance)?
- Inscription on the photo: level B for the text; whether the text names the person shown or the sender is a separate question.
- Family identification: level E, with the name of the person who made it.
- Resemblance between photos (face, ears, posture): at most H. Never let a resemblance-based identification carry other facts (dates, uniforms, places).
- Uniforms, studio stamps, card formats and clothing date a photo only roughly. Name the feature and the source you used to interpret it; otherwise say "possibly".
- Keep the identification reversible: store it as a separate field (who, basis, date), not inside the caption text.


## 9. Checking other people's online trees

Online trees are leads, not sources. Warning signs that a tree merged different people:

- two spouses at the same time, or a marriage after the recorded death;
- very many children, or births when a parent was a child, over 50 (mother) or already dead;
- birth dates for the same person that differ by more than about five years;
- a common surname in a large place, with no parents or places that separate namesakes.

Look at who changed the entry and when (change log), and trace each fact back to an attached record before using it.
