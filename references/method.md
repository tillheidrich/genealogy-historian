# Method: source criticism and proof

## 1. Terms

- **Source**: the physical or digital object (register volume, index card, newspaper page, photo, letter, recording).
- **Information**: what the source says on a specific point.
- **Evidence**: information interpreted as an answer to a research question.

Classify each source:

- **Original** (first recording, or a scan of it) vs. **derivative** (transcript, abstract, index, database entry, family tree online, AI transcription). Derivatives point you to originals; they never replace them.
- **Authored work** (chronicle, local history book, earlier family tree): useful for leads, cite as secondary.

Classify each piece of information:

- **Primary**: from someone with first-hand knowledge (the father reporting a birth, the clerk recording a marriage he witnessed).
- **Secondary**: second-hand (age at death given by a relative, birthplace in a death record).
- **Undetermined**: informant unknown.

Classify evidence:

- **Direct**: answers the question by itself ("born 15 July 1890 in X").
- **Indirect**: answers it only in combination with other information (a boy aged 12 in 1902 who is the stepson of a known man).
- **Negative**: the absence of something where it should be (no entry in a complete register). Only valid when the search and the completeness of the source are documented.

## 2. Historical source criticism

Follow the classical steps (Droysen, Bernheim) for every important source.

**External criticism (is the source what it claims to be?)**
- Who created it, when, where, for what purpose?
- Is it the original, a copy, a later transcription? Are pages missing?
- Does format, handwriting, paper, stamp fit the time?
- How did it reach us (archive holding, family estate, online platform)?

**Internal criticism (can we believe what it says?)**
- Was the author in a position to know? Was he there?
- Did the author have an interest in shaping the content (official reports, applications, court files, letters to authorities, self-presentation)?
- How long after the event was it written?
- Is it consistent with itself and with other independent sources?
- Typical distortions: ages rounded or wrong, names heard and spelled by clerks, places given as the nearest larger town, euphemisms in obituaries, political language in Nazi-era or wartime records.

**Context**
- Which rules applied (civil registration from 1874 in Prussia and 1876 in the whole German Reich, earlier in the Rhineland under French law)?
- Which pressures applied (war, occupation, party membership drives, censorship)?

## 3. Proof standard

Adapted from the Genealogical Proof Standard. A conclusion is proven only when:

1. a reasonably exhaustive search has been made (all sources that could reasonably hold the answer, including the obvious negative checks);
2. every source is cited completely;
3. the evidence is analysed and correlated (each point tested against the others);
4. conflicts are resolved with a stated reason, not ignored;
5. the conclusion is written out with its reasoning.

If one of these is missing, the conclusion is at most "very likely".

## 4. Evidence levels

Use one level per statement. The level describes the **weakest** element the statement needs, not the best source cited. A sentence that combines a register entry with a family story is an E statement, not a U statement. Better: split it into atomic statements, each with its own level and source.

| Level | Meaning |
|---|---|
| U | Certificate or register entry (civil or church), scan read |
| R | Marginal note on a register entry (later marriage, death, name change) |
| A | Archive card or file of a public archive, scan read, matched by name, date and place |
| Z | Newspaper, gazette, address book or other print, scan read; matching confidence stated separately |
| F | Family document (chronicle, typed family notes, family Bible) not yet checked against registers |
| B | Inscription, stamp or print on a photo or note |
| E | Memory or story told by family (recording, interview, user statement). Note the chain if it is second-hand ("Till, after Berthold") |
| L | Literature, reference work, forum post, online index or database entry (for example a FamilySearch or Verlustlisten index, a local history book, a forum transcription). The original was not seen; use it as a finding aid and to corroborate |
| H | Hypothesis: a conclusion drawn from several indications, not documented |
| O | Open: sources conflict or are missing |

A statement can rise from E or F to U once the register entry is found. Record the change and keep the old source.

Rules that keep levels honest:

- **Matching sets the level too.** A card that gives a name and an address but no parents does not prove a family relationship. The card content is A; the relationship drawn from it is H until a second source confirms it.
- **AI readings are derivatives.** OCR, handwriting recognition and transcriptions by an AI model (including this one) are finding aids. Mark them "read by AI, not yet checked by a person" until someone has checked the scan.
- **Context needs its own source.** Background statements (what a camp was like, when a company hired foreigners, how many people did X) need a citation like any other fact. Do not invent figures, percentages or odds ("99.5 percent", "two to one").
- **Shared online trees are not sources.** FamilySearch Family Tree, Ancestry member trees, Geni, MyHeritage trees: use them only for leads, never cite them as evidence. Their attached record images are sources once you read them.

## 5. Confidence

For matches between a record and a person, add one of: proven, very likely, possible, unclear. Define them as in SKILL.md and do not mix them with the levels. A newspaper item can be level Z and "very likely" at the same time. Store the confidence as its own field next to the level, not only in the running text, so that summaries cannot drop it.

## 5a. Cross-checks that are easy to forget

Run these every time a new record comes in:

- **Ages against birth dates.** Compute the age the record implies and compare it with the known birth date. A difference is a conflict to record, even if it is "probably OCR".
- **Dates against other dates.** A second-hand date (forum, book) that contradicts a dated newspaper or register is a conflict, not a correction.
- **Two records of the same person.** If two cards give different birth dates or places, record both and say why you still merge them.
- **Superlatives and counts** ("the oldest mother", "six people born in X") must be recomputed from the data, not remembered.

## 6. Conflicts

When sources disagree:

1. Quote both exactly.
2. Rank them: original over derivative, primary over secondary, earlier over later, neutral over interested, specific over vague.
3. Look for an explanation (clerical error, rounding, second person of the same name, deliberate false statement).
4. Write the result: "Birth date 15 July 1890 (U, birth register); a 1914 prisoner card gives 16 July 1890 (A), a 1916 newspaper gives age 20 instead of 25 (Z). The register is preferred."

## 7. Negative evidence

Log every negative search:

- date, database or archive, holding, years and places covered, query and variants used, result;
- how complete the source is (for example "about 20 percent of the cards are lost", "the register for 1829 does not survive").

Write conclusions carefully: "Not found in the NSDAP central and regional card files" is a finding; "was not a member" is not.

## 7a. Cluster research (FAN method)

When a person is hard to find, research the people around them: Friends, Associates, Neighbours. Godparents, marriage witnesses, informants on death records, neighbours in address books, fellow passengers, fellow internees. They often come from the same village, and their records name places the target's records leave out. Keep a small list of these names per person and search them like the target.

## 8. Avoiding bias

- Write down the alternative explanations before you search, not after.
- For every promising hit, look for the one detail that would prove it wrong.
- Do not weigh evidence by how good the story would be.
- Distinguish what the user hopes from what the sources say, and say so politely when they differ.

## 9. Writing results

- State facts with date, place, level and source.
- Mark interpretation as interpretation ("this suggests", "the most likely reading is").
- Separate "what the source says" from "what it means for us".
- End with open questions and the concrete next step for each.
