# Family stories and interviews

## 1. Status

- A family story is a source of level E. It is valuable and often has a true core, but dates, places, names and causes shift over generations.
- Record who told it, when, to whom, and in which words. Second-hand stories ("my uncle said") are weaker than first-hand ones.
- Never turn a story into a fact without a document. Show story and evidence side by side.

## 2. Testing a story

1. Split the story into checkable claims (who, what, where, when).
2. For each claim, ask which records would exist if it were true.
3. Search those records. Record hits and negatives.
4. Check the claim against what was possible at the time.
5. Write the result per claim: confirmed, partly confirmed, implausible, open.

Example (anonymised): "He was in France when the First World War began, was held prisoner, somehow got free, later worked on building the Suez Canal, and that is how he avoided the war."

- In France in 1914 and held prisoner: confirmed. Red Cross cards show a civilian internee; newspapers report two escape attempts. Level A and Z.
- Got free: confirmed in a different form. He was repatriated in November 1918, not freed early.
- Avoided the war: consistent. A civilian internee was not conscripted.
- Built the Suez Canal: implausible as stated. The canal was built 1859–1869, before his birth. It was widened until the 1920s and employed European skilled workers, but Germans were excluded from Egypt from 1914 until about 1922/23. Work there is possible before 1914 or after about 1923. Open.

## 3. Interviews with witnesses

- Ask open questions first ("What did your father tell you about France?"), then specific ones ("Do you remember the name of the camp?").
- Show photos and documents and ask about them; write down who is pictured according to whom.
- Ask how the person knows: lived it, was told it, read it.
- Record date, place and permission. Keep recordings and transcripts.
- Prepare questions that a document has raised, and say which document.
- Be gentle with painful topics (war, death of children, Nazi era). The witness decides what is told and what is shared.

## 4. Writing

- "According to his grandson (2026), he worked in a bicycle shop in France (E). Before 1914 he lived in Paris (Z, newspaper 1916). The shop is not yet documented."


## Interview protocol

For every conversation keep: date, place, who was present, who asked, whether it was recorded (file name), consent to record and to use it, and which photos or documents were shown. Without this, a memory cannot be cited.

## Asking without leading

Ask open questions and do not put the answer into the question.

- Leading: "Berthold says your father worked in a bicycle shop in Paris. When was that?"
- Open: "What did your father tell you about his time in France? Where did he live, and what did he do for a living?"

Show the photo first and ask "Who is this?" before you say who you think it is.

## Second-hand stories

When a story reaches you through someone else (the user tells you what a cousin said), record the chain: "Till, after Berthold, 03.11.2026". Second-hand memory is weaker than first-hand memory; ask the original teller when possible.
